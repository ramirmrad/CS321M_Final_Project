Overview
You may work in groups of up to three people (updated April 5) to complete a final project exploring a topic related to AI measurement science. Your project should demonstrate understanding of course concepts such as probabilistic measurement models, reliability analysis, validity reasoning, and evaluation design. Projects may involve developing new measurement methods, analyzing evaluation datasets, or studying the reliability and validity of existing benchmarks.

Make it Yours: Measurement problems are contextual. Choose a domain, dataset, or evaluation problem that interests you and use it to explore ideas from AI measurement science.

Deliverables & Grading
Your group will submit the following via gradescope
Pre-Analysis Plan (30%)
Final Manuscript (60%)
Code (10%)
All submissions should be submitted as PDF. You must also submit code and data for reproducibility (Exceptions are possible for sensitive data and code, please discuss with the course staff).

Topic Selection
You are free to choose any topic that relates to AI Measurement Science. Possible directions include:
Applying or extending probabilistic measurement models (e.g., Rasch, 2PL/3PL, Bradley–Terry, factor models) for AI evaluation
Studying latent capability estimation from sparse evaluation matrices
Predicting missing model–benchmark results from partial evaluation data
Amortized or scalable inference for large evaluation datasets
Comparing item-wise vs. pairwise evaluation models
Empirical analysis of scaling laws in evaluation performance
Quantifying the reliability of LLM-as-a-judge systems
Applying Generalizability Theory or random-effects models to evaluation pipelines
Studying annotation noise in human preference data
Estimating uncertainty in model rankings
Diagnosing sources of measurement error across benchmarks
Analyzing the construct validity of existing benchmarks
Studying correlations between benchmarks measuring similar capabilities
Identifying benchmark contamination or memorization effects
Designing new evaluation tasks targeting specific capabilities
Investigating benchmark saturation or score inflation
Active learning strategies for selecting evaluation items
Designing synthetic benchmarks or adversarial tests
Methods for detecting or filtering problematic evaluation items
Sampling strategies for efficient evaluation
Red-teaming or stress-testing evaluation datasets
Evaluating AI systems in specific domains (education, healthcare, coding, etc.)
Analyzing large-scale evaluation datasets (e.g., Chatbot Arena or open benchmark collections)
Studying the robustness of leaderboard rankings
Measurement approaches for agentic or tool-using AI systems

Documentation (5%)

Usability (1%) (unless we have agreed otherwise for some specialized reason)

Evaluation Criteria
We will assess:
Can we set up the environment using your instructions?
Can we reproduce your main results within a reasonable tolerance?
Is the code well-organized and understandable?
Are the methods clearly implemented and documented?
Note: While we don't expect production-quality code, we do expect research-quality code that allows others to understand, verify, and build upon your work. This is essential for scientific reproducibility and is increasingly expected in ML research.
Tips for Code Submission
Start organizing your code early, don't wait until the deadline
Test your setup instructions on a fresh environment (ask your partner or a classmate to try)
Use version control throughout your project, not just at the end
Comment as you code, not retrospectively
Include a "quickstart" option that runs a small-scale version of your experiments
For computationally expensive experiments, consider providing pre-computed results alongside the code to reproduce them
Code Reuse and Attribution
Clearly mark any code adapted from external sources with comments
Include licenses for any third-party code you use
If you build on existing implementations, explain your modifications
Original implementation is valued, but thoughtful adaptation and extension is also acceptable when properly attributed

Tips for Success
For individuals: Choose a well-scoped question you can explore thoroughly. Consider empirical analysis of existing methods or focused theoretical contribution.
For larger teams: Leverage complementary skills (e.g., one person focuses on implementation, another on experiments; or split theoretical vs. empirical work). Meet regularly and maintain clear communication.
General advice:
Start early, especially on data collection/generation
Use course concepts explicitly (this demonstrates learning)
When stuck, come to office hours
Use the pre-analysis plan as an opportunity to get feedback before heavy experimental work
Iterate on your ideas based on feedback
Make your code reproducible from day one
Don't overscope – better to do one thing well than three things poorly
